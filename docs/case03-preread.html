Pain

About 96 enquiries a month wait two days or more, and that wait cuts conversion by up to four times. A third land when nobody is at the desk. The ones that do reach a designer arrive as a bare forwarded message, so the first call repeats questions the front desk already asked.

User

The designer who receives the lead, and the caller who wants an answer tonight, not tomorrow. Nikhil is the owner and buyer, and the front desk (two people, 10–7) is relieved of the repetitive part.

Outcome

Every call is answered in seconds, day or night. Qualified leads reach a designer within 5 minutes of the call ending, with all five criteria already answered. Nikhil sees volume, conversion, response time and running cost without pulling numbers by hand.

Deliverable 2

Nine checks

Each check is tested against evidence from the case. Eight pass, some with conditions on how the build works. One stops a feature.

01

Is the pain real and measured?

Pass

Nikhil pulled the numbers himself: 48% unanswered in 48 hours, a 4× conversion gap, 33% after hours. The transcripts show it happening: T08 missed at night, T16 lost in a hand-off, W05 lost over a weekend.

02

Is there enough volume, and does it repeat?

Pass

About 200 enquiries a month, roughly 7 a day, and the brief names the phone as the channel with the most volume and urgency. Every qualifying call follows the same five-question script.

03

Is the input available to a machine?

Pass

A voice platform (Vaani) produces a live transcript. Callers volunteer the facts in plain speech: "3BHK in Kothrud, about 1,400 sq ft carpet, done by March."

04

Are the rules written down?

Pass

qualified.md sets out five criteria and what to do at each boundary. services.md lists the service-area localities and the 6-week minimum lead time. pricing.md gives the exact deflection sentence.

So: the rules live in code (lib/gates.ts), not in the prompt. The model reports facts and code makes the decision, so a persuasive caller can't talk the model past a rule.

05

Can we test it against the real answer?

Pass

The 20 September calls come with what the front desk actually decided. Replayed through the system they score 21/21 (T17 counts as two calls), and 18 offline tests cover each gate.

06

What does a wrong answer cost?

Pass, with a rule

Wrongly declining a lead loses a ₹8–14 L project. Wrongly forwarding one costs a designer about 20 minutes. The two errors are not symmetric.

So: the system declines only on a clear failure. Unclear timeline, budget or decision-maker goes forward with a note, and unclear scope or location gets one question (qualified.md: "do not push"). Every decline is logged on the dashboard for review.

07

Where must a human stay?

Pass, with a rule

T09 is an existing client whose designer hasn't replied in five days and who wants Nikhil. An agent cannot fix that. Design advice and the price conversation also belong to the designer.

So: complaints are flagged ESCALATE and emailed to Nikhil, and the caller is promised a senior call-back within 15 minutes. The agent qualifies and books, and people do the rest.

08

Could it say something it must not?

Stops a feature

pricing.md: "No number from this guide should be quoted to a client." The guide is marked indicative only, and the spread is large: T13's desk says a premium kitchen can cost three times a standard one.

So: the per-sq-ft quote is cut (see below). Pricing numbers are kept out of the agent's prompt, and a post-call guardrail flags any ₹ figure the agent says.

09

Is it worth running, and can we see the cost?

Pass

On free tiers it costs $0.00. At paid list rates it would be about $0.43 per qualified lead (assuming $0.05/min for voice plus Gemini tokens), against a ₹8–14 L project. Nikhil asked to see the tool's own cost, so every call writes a cost row and the dashboard shows billed and paid-rate cost side by side.

The Cut · stopped by check 08

The agent will not quote "our standard per-square-foot pricing"

"And the system should be able to quote our standard per-square-foot pricing so people don't have to wait for that." — Nikhil (the brief's prompt says Arjun)

Nikhil's own pricing guide forbids it, and the guide's range runs from standard to luxury at about twice the price. Any number spoken on a call would be wrong for most callers, and it would anchor the negotiation before a designer has seen the site. The transcripts also show it isn't needed: T02 and T13 both pushed for a ballpark, were deflected, and booked anyway.

What we build instead serves the real goal, which is that nobody waits for a price. The agent uses the exact deflection line and books the consultation during the call, where the designer gives a real number. The handoff tells the designer "asked about pricing, come ready to talk numbers."

6
Decision · Telegram or email

Gmail, with a filter that makes it urgent

  • The studio already runs on email. Client threads, vendor quotes and site photos are there. A lead becomes a thread the designer can reply to, forward or find six months later. In a Telegram group, lead number 40 is buried under the 39 before it.
  • Free, with no setup. A Gmail App Password allows about 500 sends a day, against roughly 200 calls a month, and needs no domain or DNS work.
  • Urgency comes from the subject line. Every subject starts [Aangan Lead], [Aangan URGENT], [Aangan Follow-up] or [Aangan Call-back]. A one-line Gmail filter marks these Important, so the phone notifies for leads only.
  • Three audiences: designers, Nikhil and the front desk. Nobody is emailed about a declined call.

Trade-off accepted: a Telegram ping arrives a few seconds sooner. The 4× gain is measured against the next day, though, not the next minute. An email flagged Important and read within the hour captures the gain and keeps the lead where the project will be run. Telegram stays wired in as the fallback.
