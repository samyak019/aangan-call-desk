Pain

About 96 enquiries a month wait two days or more, and that wait cuts conversion by up to four times. A third land when nobody is at the desk. The ones that do reach a designer arrive as a bare forwarded message, so the first call repeats questions the front desk already asked.

User

The designer who receives the lead, and the caller who wants an answer tonight, not tomorrow. Nikhil is the owner and buyer, and the front desk (two people, 10–7) is relieved of the repetitive part.

Outcome

Every call is answered in seconds, day or night. Qualified leads reach a designer within 5 minutes of the call ending, with all five criteria already answered. Nikhil sees volume, conversion, response time and running cost without pulling numbers by hand.

Deliverable 2

Nine checks: does this deserve to be built?

Each answer uses evidence from the brief, the three studio files and the September transcripts.

Kill switches

Any No means don't build it.
01

Problem real?

Yes

Nikhil pulled his own numbers: about 48% of roughly 200 enquiries a month get no reply within 48 hours. A third arrive outside 10am–7pm. An answer within an hour converts at 4× the next-day rate. The transcripts show the leaks happening: T08 rang at 10:47pm and nobody answered. T16 was promised a call-back that never came and asked whether that's "a good sign if you forget before you even start". T17's line dropped mid-sentence.

02

Workflow repeated?

Yes

Every phone enquiry runs the same script. The desk asks what the caller wants done, where, when, how big and who decides, then books or declines. Nikhil wrote those five questions down in qualified.md. That's about 7 enquiries a day, every day, including the third that arrive when nobody is at the desk.

03

Input available?

Yes

The caller provides everything by speaking: "3BHK in Kothrud, about 1,400 sq ft carpet, done by March." The voice platform (Vaani) turns the call into a transcript. The rules are already written down: service areas and the 6-week minimum in services.md, the five criteria in qualified.md, and the pricing script in pricing.md. One gap: callers rarely give an email address, so the booking confirmation goes to the studio and the designer uses the phone number.

Sizing

Any No means build something smaller.
04

Output valuable?

Yes

This is the second half of Nikhil's ask: the lead should reach a designer "with everything already asked". The handoff note answers all five criteria and adds the referral source, whether they asked about price, and their mood. The designer's first call starts at the design brief, not the questions the front desk already asked. Declined callers get an honest answer on the call, so they aren't left waiting for a call-back.

05

Impact measurable?

Yes

The dashboard tracks the numbers he pulled by hand. Unanswered within 48 hours: from 48% to the share of calls the agent misses. Call end to designer: from a day or more to a target under 5 minutes (about 9 seconds in replay). It also tracks after-hours calls caught, qualified leads, consultations booked, and decline reasons by gate. Replayed on September's calls, the system matches the studio's own decision on 21 of 21.

08

ROI worth it?

Yes

It runs on free tiers (Vaani, Gemini, Supabase, Cal.com, HubSpot, Gmail and Vercel), so the billed cost is $0.00. At paid rates the dashboard estimates about $0.43 per qualified lead, against a ₹8–14 L average project. One extra project converted a year pays for it many times over. The cost is shown on the dashboard, as Nikhil asked.

Boundary

Any No means put a human exactly here.
06

Failure risk OK?

Only with a guardrail

The two failures don't cost the same. Wrongly declining a good lead loses a ₹8–14 L project. Wrongly forwarding a weak one costs a designer about 20 minutes. So the system declines only on a clear failure (Nashik, advice only, a 3-week deadline, a ₹1.5 L budget for a flat, a restaurant). Anything unclear goes to a person: unclear timeline, budget or decision-maker reaches the designer with a note, and unclear scope or location goes to the front desk with one question. Every decline is logged on the dashboard where Nikhil can review it.

07

Judgment protected?

No for pricing, so a human holds it

Qualifying a caller follows written rules, so the agent can do it. Pricing can't be reduced to rules. pricing.md says: "Pricing varies by site, materials specification, and project complexity… All pricing is confirmed by the assigned designer at the consultation." T13's front desk adds that a premium kitchen can cost three times a standard one. The gates are written in code, and Gemini only extracts facts, so the rules don't change with how a caller words things. Complaints such as T09 need Nikhil's judgment, so they are escalated to him with a senior call-back within 15 minutes.

09

Owner clear?

Yes

I own the system for Nikhil in the founder's office: the rules file, the agent prompt and the dashboard. Designers own every lead from the moment the email lands, including the consultation and the price. Nikhil owns escalations and reviews the decline reasons. The front desk owns call-backs and one-question follow-ups.

The Cut

Check 07 rules out "quote our standard per-square-foot pricing so people don't have to wait for that." Pricing is a designer's call, made after seeing the site and the materials. Nikhil's own guide says no number should be quoted. A rate read out on the phone would be wrong for most callers and would anchor the negotiation before a designer is involved. Check 06 points the same way: one confident wrong quote does more damage than making someone wait a day.

The transcripts show the quote isn't what wins the client. T02 and T13 both pushed for a ballpark, were deflected, and booked anyway. So the agent gives Nikhil's exact deflection line and books the consultation during the call, and the designer gives the number there.

To Nikhil: "It won't quote your per-square-foot rate. It will make sure nobody waits for one. Every pricing question ends with a consultation booked on the same call."

6
Decision · Telegram or email

Gmail, with a filter that makes it urgent

  • The studio already runs on email. Client threads, vendor quotes and site photos are there. A lead becomes a thread the designer can reply to, forward or find six months later. In a Telegram group, lead number 40 is buried under the 39 before it.
  • Free, with no setup. A Gmail App Password allows about 500 sends a day, against roughly 200 calls a month, and needs no domain or DNS work.
  • Urgency comes from the subject line. Every subject starts [Aangan Lead], [Aangan URGENT], [Aangan Follow-up] or [Aangan Call-back]. A one-line Gmail filter marks these Important, so the phone notifies for leads only.
  • Three audiences: designers, Nikhil and the front desk. Nobody is emailed about a declined call.

Trade-off accepted: a Telegram ping arrives a few seconds sooner. The 4× gain is measured against the next day, though, not the next minute. An email flagged Important and read within the hour captures the gain and keeps the lead where the project will be run. Telegram stays wired in as the fallback.
